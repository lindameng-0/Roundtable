Readerfold / Blog / After the first draft

You finished the draft. Now stop fixing the first sentence.

After finishing a novel's first draft, review character goals, cause and effect, pacing, and missing context before polishing individual sentences. A structural revision pass helps a fiction writer decide which scenes need to change before investing in line edits.

You type “The End,” open chapter one, and immediately change a verb. It is a satisfying edit. It is also a very small answer to a much larger question: what is this draft actually doing to a reader?

There is nothing wrong with a cleaner sentence. The trouble starts when sentence work becomes a way to avoid looking at the whole book. You can spend an afternoon refining a scene that your next structural revision will remove.

Read the book you wrote, not the one you remember.

You know why the sister refuses the invitation. You remember the earlier version in which she explained it. You know the invitation returns at the end. A new reader has none of that knowledge.

For this pass, put the revision file aside. At the end of each scene, write two short notes: What does the reader know now? and What might they expect next? Resist explaining what you meant. Point to the detail that makes the expectation possible.

This is not a full editorial audit. It is a way to uncover the difference between the story in your head and the information on the page. A missing connection becomes easier to see when you have to name it.

Try it with one turning point.

Choose a scene that changes the direction of the story. Read the scene before it, the turn itself, and its immediate consequence. Keep your notes to three sentences.

Invented example

Before: I expect the daughter to expose her father because she has finally found his ledger.

At the turn: She burns it. I understand that she changes her mind, but I cannot yet see what changes it.

After: Her father thanks her. The scene treats the choice as reconciliation, although I read it as surrender.

There is a revision question here: what should make the burning feel like a choice she owns? That question is more useful than “make the daughter stronger.” It directs you back to a moment, an intention, and a reader’s interpretation.

Perhaps the missing reason belongs in an earlier scene. Perhaps surrender is exactly the point, and the aftermath needs to acknowledge it. You have not found an instruction. You have found a place to investigate.

Borrow a perspective before you commit to a rewrite.

If you have reread the book until every action feels inevitable, another perspective can help. Give a human reader the sequence without first explaining the intended effect. Ask what they expected before the turn and how they understood the choice afterward.

You can also explore that question in Readerfold. Submit enough of the draft to include the setup and consequence, choose AI readers with different priorities, and focus the reading on the turning point. Compare their passage-linked reactions with your own notes. An emotional reading might follow the family bond while another reader asks what the daughter stands to lose.

These are simulated perspectives, and they may miss a clue. Their value is in giving you a specific interpretation to check against your words. The annotated sample shows how that comparison can lead to an author’s decision.

Leave this pass with a question you can act on.

Write one sentence: “In the next revision, I want the reader to understand ______ before ______.” Then identify the smallest part of the manuscript that could make that happen. Fix that connection before you polish around it.

When you are ready to send the draft to people, use our beta reader questionnaire to choose a few focused questions. You do not need to ask everything at once.
