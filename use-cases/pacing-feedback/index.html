Find the pause that costs your scene its urgency.

Slow prose is not automatically a pacing problem. A pause can deepen anticipation, give a loss room to land, or interrupt a moment that needs to move. Roundtable helps you connect a reader's loss of momentum to the passage behind it.

Illustrative worked example. The original fiction, reader reactions, and revision below were created for this guide with AI assistance. They are curated examples, not a recorded Roundtable run or customer results.

Set up the readingCompare reactionsConsider a revision

Bring enough of the story

Bring the whole scene, including its immediate goal and outcome. State whether you want breathless action, slow suspense, or a deliberate recovery beat. Without that intention, advice to make everything faster can flatten the story.

Give your readers a focus

Choose Pacing and momentum for one reader and Emotional authenticity for another. Ask them to identify where anticipation grows or fades, and whether a reflective pause changes their understanding of the character.

In Roundtable

Paste your draft or upload a TXT, DOCX, or PDF. Confirm the extracted text, genre, and audience, then choose your readers and edit their focus before starting. Compare their reactions beside your manuscript; use the editorial report to consider patterns across the reading.

One passage, two readings

The last ferry sounded its horn. Inez stopped on the quay. The warehouse windows glowed amber, the color of the bottles her grandfather had lined above his stove. Every summer he had polished them with a rag. She counted the windows. The horn sounded again.

A reader following momentum

The last ferry creates a deadline. The memory and window counting put that deadline on hold. I need to know whether she is hesitating on purpose or whether the description has stopped the action.

A reader following emotion

The amber bottles make this departure feel personal. I would miss that connection if the whole memory disappeared, but I don't yet know what staying would mean to her.

Turn the disagreement into a question

Both reactions point to the same pause but assign it different value. Before cutting description, decide whether Inez's hesitation is the scene's conflict. If she wants to catch the ferry, carry the memory through an action. If she wants an excuse to stay, make that reluctance legible.

One possible revision

Original

The last ferry sounded its horn. Inez stopped on the quay. The warehouse windows glowed amber, the color of the bottles her grandfather had lined above his stove. Every summer he had polished them with a rag. She counted the windows. The horn sounded again.

Revised

The last ferry sounded its horn. Inez ran past the warehouse windows, amber as the bottles above her grandfather's stove. At the gangway she looked back once. The horn sounded again.

What the edit changes

The revision keeps one image and moves Inez toward the ferry. It sacrifices the lingering, inward rhythm and makes departure her apparent goal. It would be the wrong edit if her refusal to leave is the point of the scene.

What to check next

Use the editorial report to look for repeated delays across scenes, then return to each passage. Separate pauses that develop a choice from pauses that repeat information. Revise one scene and read it aloud to check whether the new rhythm suits the intended mood.

AI reactions are prompts for investigation, not a pacing score you must optimize. A quiet scene may be exactly what your intended readers need after an intense chapter.
