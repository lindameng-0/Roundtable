Does your opening raise the right question?

An opening can withhold the answer and still give readers their bearings. Use Readerfold to examine whether your first chapter creates curiosity about the story you mean to tell, or leaves readers unsure what is happening.

Illustrative worked example. The original fiction, reader reactions, and revision below were created for this guide with AI assistance. They are curated examples, not a recorded Readerfold run or customer results.

Bring enough of the story

Bring the opening scene through its first meaningful choice or turn. Include the genre and intended audience. A single striking sentence cannot show whether the chapter delivers on its promise.

Give your readers a focus

Give one reader Pacing and momentum and another Plot logic. In the writer focus note, ask: What question makes you want to continue, and where does missing context stop you following the action?

Paste your draft or upload a TXT, DOCX, or PDF. Confirm the extracted text, genre, and audience, then choose your readers and edit their focus before starting. Compare their reactions beside your manuscript; use the editorial report to consider patterns across the reading.

One passage, two readings

At six, Mara stole the key from her father's coat. By seven, she had returned it. At breakfast he asked whether she had slept well. She watched the garden gate and said yes.

A reader following suspense

Returning the key before breakfast makes this feel like a secret already in motion. I want to know what Mara did during that hour. The gate gives me somewhere to look for a consequence.

A reader following cause and effect

I can follow the sequence, but I don't know what the key opens. If the next scene changes location, I may lose the connection between the theft and the gate.

Turn the disagreement into a question

The passage offers a clear sequence and an unresolved action. The disagreement is about orientation, not whether every secret needs explaining. Check the next scene: if it develops the gate, the original may be enough. If it introduces another mystery, one concrete connection could keep the first question alive.

One possible revision

Original

At six, Mara stole the key from her father's coat. By seven, she had returned it. At breakfast he asked whether she had slept well. She watched the garden gate and said yes.

Revised

At six, Mara stole the garden key from her father's coat. By seven, she had returned it. At breakfast he asked whether she had slept well. She watched the gate and said yes.

What the edit changes

Naming the garden key connects the theft to the place she watches. It narrows the mystery from what the key opens to what she did outside. Keep the original if discovering the key's purpose is itself an intended reveal.

What to check next

Read the chapter again with the small clarification in place. Ask a human reader what they expect to happen next before telling them your intention. Compare that expectation with your planned story, rather than counting how many readers call the opening a strong hook.

An AI panel can suggest competing interpretations of a hook. It cannot predict whether an agent will request the manuscript or whether an audience will buy the book.
